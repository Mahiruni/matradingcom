01 · FOUNDATION

How Markets Move

Trading begins with one uncomfortable fact: the market does not owe a trader a clean pattern, a predictable sequence, or a reaction at a level simply because that level looks important. Price is the visible result of orders being matched in an environment where participants have different objectives, time horizons, information and risk limits. The first foundation lesson is therefore not about finding entries. It is about learning to describe what price is doing without immediately turning that description into a trade.

A market can be thought of as an auction. At one moment, buyers are willing to pay increasingly higher prices because they believe the asset can be worth more. At another moment, sellers become more aggressive, or buyers stop accepting higher prices, and the auction moves lower. When neither side is willing to push aggressively, price can rotate inside a range. This simple auction idea explains why markets alternate between expansion and compression. Expansion is a period where price travels efficiently from one area to another. Compression is a period where price overlaps, rotates and repeatedly tests nearby prices.

Timeframe changes the question

A chart is not a single market story. A monthly candle, H4 candle and M15 candle can all describe the same instrument while showing different structures. Higher timeframes are useful for context because they compress noise and reveal larger areas of balance, expansion and directional movement. Lower timeframes provide more detail but also contain more temporary fluctuations. This is why the MA framework separates context from execution. H4 and H1 can establish the environment, while M15 can provide the execution structure.

Imagine XAUUSD has been making higher highs and higher lows on H4. That does not mean every M15 candle must rise. A temporary M15 decline can simply be a pullback inside the higher-timeframe advance. Conversely, an attractive M15 bullish pattern is not automatically high quality if H4 is sitting directly below a major resistance area. The correct question is not “Is this candle bullish?” It is “What is the candle doing inside the larger structure?”

Impulse, pullback and range

Three basic conditions appear repeatedly. An impulse is directional movement with relatively strong displacement. A pullback is a counter-move that retraces part of an earlier impulse. A range is an area where price repeatedly trades between boundaries without sustained directional delivery. These conditions are not permanent labels. A pullback can become a reversal; a range can become an accumulation or distribution area; an impulse can fail and return into its origin.

Practical observation

Before opening a position, describe the last meaningful H4/H1 movement in one sentence. Example: “Price expanded upward, then entered a corrective pullback toward a previous demand area.” This forces observation before prediction.

Sessions and volatility

Market behaviour also changes with participation. Different trading sessions can have different liquidity and volatility characteristics. A quiet period may produce overlapping candles, while a major session transition can produce rapid expansion. Scheduled economic events can create abnormal movement. This matters because a setup that appears technically clean can be invalidated by a sudden volatility event. A disciplined trader therefore knows when the market is likely to behave differently rather than assuming every candle has the same meaning.

The connection to the MA method

This foundation leads directly into the MA process. We do not begin with Fibonacci because Fibonacci needs a meaningful impulse. We do not begin with a sweep because a sweep needs a meaningful liquidity reference. We do not begin with an entry because an entry needs an invalidation. The sequence exists because each decision depends on the information established by the previous one.

The objective of this lesson is therefore not to predict every move. It is to build a neutral market-reading habit. Mark the higher-timeframe direction, identify whether price is expanding or rotating, locate the current leg, and only then move to structure and liquidity. When this becomes automatic, the chart becomes less visually overwhelming because every new candle has a role within a larger context.

Finally, remember that market movement is probabilistic. Two identical-looking structures can produce different outcomes. Your advantage cannot come from believing that one pattern guarantees the next candle. It must come from defining conditions, controlling risk, recording results and repeating a process that can be evaluated objectively.

02 · PRICE ACTION

Charts & Candles

A candlestick is a compact record of an auction during a chosen period. The open shows where that period began, the high and low show the extremes reached, and the close shows where the period finished. The candle therefore contains more information than its color. A bullish candle tells you the close is above the open; a bearish candle tells you the opposite. The size of the body, the length of the wicks and the candle's position relative to surrounding candles provide additional information.

The most important habit is to wait for the candle to close when your strategy depends on closed-candle confirmation. During formation, a candle can trade above a previous high and later close below it. If you treat the temporary high as confirmed information, you may build a decision around something that disappears before the candle finishes. This is particularly important for structure shifts and liquidity sweeps.

Body versus wick

The body represents the distance between open and close. A large body indicates that the candle finished materially away from its opening price. A wick shows that price traded beyond the body and then returned. A long upper wick after a liquidity raid can indicate rejection, but it does not automatically prove that sellers control the next move. Context matters. The same wick can mean very different things at the middle of a random range and at a well-defined external liquidity pool.

Consider a previous swing high. If price trades above that high and then closes back below it, the event may be described as a sweep or failed breakout. But a trader should not stop at the label. Ask what happened next. Did price displace downward? Did it break meaningful internal structure? Did it return to a planned area? Did the higher-timeframe context support the idea? These questions convert candle reading into a process.

Impulse candles and displacement

When price moves with urgency, candles may become larger and overlap less. In the MA framework, displacement is useful because it can provide evidence that the market has changed from balance to directional delivery. However, large candles also create risk. Chasing a candle after it has already expanded can produce poor location. The purpose of identifying displacement is often to wait for a controlled retracement or a better-defined entry, not to press the buy or sell button simply because the candle looks powerful.

Reading sequences rather than isolated candles

One candle rarely contains enough information. A sequence is more useful. For example, price can compress into a narrow range, sweep one boundary, print rejection, then produce a strong candle through internal structure. The sequence communicates a story: compression, liquidity event, rejection, confirmation. This is much more meaningful than saying “the red candle is a sell candle.”

Similarly, a series of higher highs and higher lows is a structural sequence, not a collection of unrelated green candles. The trader's task is to identify the meaningful swing points that organize the sequence. This leads directly to market structure.

Common candle-reading mistakes

  • Entering before the candle closes when the setup requires confirmation.
  • Treating candle color as direction without location.
  • Calling every long wick a liquidity sweep.
  • Chasing large displacement candles after the best price has already passed.
  • Ignoring the higher timeframe and reading M15 in isolation.

Charts should make your decision more precise, not more emotional. A good chart note might say: “M15 closed back below the prior high after a buy-side raid; bearish displacement followed and internal structure shifted.” That sentence is testable. “Looks bearish” is not. The difference between the two is the difference between an observation and a feeling.

03 · MARKET STRUCTURE

Market Structure

Market structure is the organization of meaningful highs and lows. It gives a trader a framework for describing direction without relying on vague impressions. In a basic bullish sequence, price creates higher highs and higher lows. In a bearish sequence, price creates lower lows and lower highs. But real markets are not perfectly stair-stepped. Internal fluctuations occur inside larger swings, and this is why structure must be separated into context and execution.

A meaningful swing is not simply the highest pixel on the screen. It should have enough surrounding price action to demonstrate that it mattered to the auction. If a tiny M15 wick is treated as the same kind of swing as a major H1 turning point, the chart becomes full of conflicting signals. The MA approach therefore emphasizes real swing highs and lows when applying Fibonacci and evaluating shifts.

External versus internal structure

External structure describes the larger movement that matters to the current context. Internal structure describes the smaller movements inside that larger leg. Suppose H1 is bullish and price is pulling back. On M15, the pullback may contain several lower lows and lower highs. Those bearish internal swings do not automatically mean H1 has reversed. The trader needs to know which structural point would actually invalidate the larger bullish thesis.

This distinction prevents a common mistake: changing bias every time the lower timeframe prints a countertrend sequence. A pullback can be bearish internally while remaining part of a bullish external structure. The execution timeframe is used to find a transition inside the larger idea, not to erase the higher-timeframe context unnecessarily.

Break of structure and shift

A structure break should be defined before it occurs. If a trader waits until price moves and then chooses whichever high or low makes the move look meaningful, the definition becomes subjective. A useful process is to identify the swing that separates the current leg from the next meaningful leg, then wait for a closed candle to confirm the break when closed-candle confirmation is part of the rules.

A shift is especially important in a reversal or pullback-entry model. Price may first raid liquidity in the direction of the old move, then displace in the opposite direction and break internal structure. The combination is more informative than either event alone. A sweep without a shift can simply be continuation. A shift without meaningful location can be a random fluctuation. The framework becomes stronger when location, liquidity and structure agree.

Structure and Fibonacci

Fibonacci is not a replacement for structure. It is applied after a meaningful impulse has been identified. In the MA model, the impulse is mapped with 1 at the origin and 0 at the extreme. This orientation is important because the 0.71 area becomes a defined retracement location rather than an arbitrary Fibonacci line.

The trader should therefore avoid drawing Fibonacci on every visible micro-swing. First identify the real impulse that matters to the setup. Then identify the origin and extreme. Then evaluate whether the 0.71 area overlaps a meaningful location and whether the expected reaction would invalidate beyond the selected stop area. The tool becomes useful because structure gave it context.

Structure is not certainty

Every structure model can fail. A closed candle can break a level and then reverse. A higher high can be followed by a deep retracement. The purpose of structure is not to eliminate uncertainty; it is to make uncertainty measurable. Once invalidation is defined, the trader knows when the original interpretation is no longer valid.

The practical exercise is simple: open historical charts and mark only the largest meaningful swings first. Then progressively add internal swings. Compare what the chart looks like with ten marks versus fifty marks. If every tiny movement is treated as a structural event, the framework becomes unusable. Good structure analysis is selective.

04 · LIQUIDITY

Liquidity

Liquidity is one of the most important and most misused words in trading education. In this course, it should not be treated as a mystical force that “must be taken.” A more disciplined definition is that liquidity refers to the availability and concentration of orders that can facilitate transactions. On a chart, traders often use obvious highs, lows, equal highs, equal lows and previous extremes as practical references because many market participants may respond around those locations.

The key idea is visibility. A level that is obvious to many traders can become a location where stops, breakout orders or resting interest cluster. Price may move through such a level and then return. That event is often called a liquidity sweep. But a sweep is an observation, not a complete trading signal.

Buy-side and sell-side references

Above a clear prior high, traders may describe the area as buy-side liquidity because short positions can have protective buy stops there and breakout participants can place buy orders above the level. Below a clear prior low, the corresponding area is often called sell-side liquidity. These labels help organize the chart, but they should not be interpreted as proof that the market will always take one side before moving to the other.

Equal highs and equal lows can be particularly useful because repeated tests make a level visually obvious. A trader can mark the level, wait to see how price behaves when it reaches it, and then look for confirmation. The important point is to avoid deciding in advance that the level must be swept. Sometimes price breaks and continues. Sometimes it sweeps and reverses. Sometimes it barely reacts.

What makes a sweep meaningful?

A meaningful sweep usually has context. Consider three pieces: location, event and reaction. Location asks whether the liquidity is meaningful relative to the higher-timeframe structure. Event asks whether price actually traded through or interacted with the level. Reaction asks whether price produced evidence afterward, such as rejection, displacement or a structure shift.

This three-part model prevents the common mistake of selling every wick above a high. A wick is not enough. If price trades above a high, closes above it and continues higher, the event may be breakout continuation rather than reversal. If price trades above it, closes back below, then displaces downward and breaks internal structure at a meaningful location, the bearish case has more evidence.

Liquidity and AMD

AMD gives liquidity a larger narrative. During accumulation, price may rotate and establish obvious boundaries. Those boundaries can attract attention. During manipulation, price can raid one side of the range. During distribution, price can expand away from the range. This is not a claim that every range follows the same sequence. It is a framework for investigating whether the observed behaviour fits the hypothesis.

Do not chase the sweep

The most important practical lesson is that the sweep itself may occur quickly. If the plan requires confirmation, the trader should not enter simply because the level was touched. Wait for the defined reaction. If the reaction does not occur, the setup is incomplete. This principle also protects against hindsight: after a large reversal, it is easy to draw a line under the wick and declare the sweep obvious. In real time, the next candles were unknown.

Liquidity analysis should therefore produce a conditional statement: “If price reaches this external high, sweeps it, rejects and confirms a structure shift, I will evaluate the short model.” That is different from “price will take this high and reverse.” The first is a plan; the second is a prediction.

05 · RISK MANAGEMENT

Risk Management

Risk management is not a final checkbox added after analysis. It determines whether a trading method can survive a sequence of losses. A strategy can have attractive entries and still fail as a business process if position size is inconsistent, stops are moved emotionally, or a trader increases risk after losses. The objective is to make one trade relatively unimportant to the account while allowing a repeatable process to operate over a large sample.

The first concept is invalidation. Before entering, identify the price condition that would make the trade thesis wrong. That level is not chosen because it creates a comfortable monetary loss; it is chosen because the market structure or setup logic would no longer support the idea. Once invalidation is established, position size can be calculated around it.

Risk percentage

If an account risks 1% per trade, a losing trade reduces equity by approximately 1% before costs and execution effects. A 0.5% risk means the same setup has half the monetary exposure. Smaller risk can be especially useful while learning because the trader can collect a meaningful sample without allowing a short losing streak to dominate the account.

In the MA framework, a trader may choose a predefined risk level such as 0.25%, 0.50% or 1%. The critical part is choosing it before execution and applying it consistently. The percentage should not suddenly become 2% because the setup “looks perfect,” nor should it become larger after a losing streak to recover losses quickly.

Stop loss is structural

The MA model uses a defined Fibonacci-based stop concept around the 0.95 level for the selected impulse. The number itself is not a universal market law. It is a rule of the model. The trader must still understand what the level represents: if price reaches the invalidation area, the chosen impulse and setup interpretation no longer provide the expected entry condition. A stop should not be widened simply because price is approaching it.

Reward and expectation

Risk-reward is useful, but it should not be confused with win rate. A strategy with a large theoretical reward relative to risk can still lose frequently. A strategy with a higher win rate can still lose money if average losses are much larger than average wins. What matters is expectancy over a sufficiently large sample, after realistic costs and execution.

For example, if a setup risks one unit and targets three units, one winner can offset three full losses before costs. But this does not mean the trader should force every chart into a three-unit target. Targets should correspond to logical market objectives and the strategy rules. In the MA model, TP1 at 0 and TP2 at -0.21 are defined objectives tied to the Fibonacci framework. The trader should test how often price reaches each objective rather than assuming it will.

Drawdown and behaviour

Losses are unavoidable in probabilistic trading. A professional process therefore plans for losing sequences before they happen. If five consecutive losses at 1% occur, the account does not become meaningless, but the psychological pressure can be significant. At 0.25%, the same sequence has a much smaller financial effect. The correct risk level depends on the trader's tested edge, capital and tolerance, but the principle is universal: survival comes before optimization.

Non-negotiable rule

Never increase risk merely because the previous trade lost. The next setup has no obligation to recover the previous loss.

Risk management also includes correlation, news exposure, spread and execution. Trading several highly correlated positions can create hidden concentration. A stop can experience slippage during fast markets. Spread can expand around important events. These factors mean that a theoretical backtest is not automatically the same as live execution.

The goal is not to remove risk. It is to define it. A trader who knows exactly how much can be lost, where the thesis fails, and what conditions justify a trade is operating a process. A trader who enters first and decides those things afterward is operating an emotion.

06 · EXECUTION

Building a Trading Plan

A trading plan converts knowledge into repeatable behaviour. Without a plan, a trader can understand market structure, liquidity and Fibonacci and still make inconsistent decisions because each chart is interpreted differently. The purpose of the plan is to define what qualifies, what does not qualify, how the trade is entered, where it is invalidated, and how the result will be recorded.

The MA plan begins with higher-timeframe context. H4 and H1 establish the broad environment. The trader asks whether price is expanding, correcting or ranging, then identifies meaningful areas where a reaction could matter. M15 is used for execution. M5 can be used as an optional confirmation layer, but lower timeframe detail should not override the main context.

The setup sequence

First comes bias. Second comes location. Third comes confirmation. Confirmation can involve a liquidity sweep, a structure shift, displacement or a combination defined by the setup. Fourth comes the valid swing used for Fibonacci. The impulse is mapped from 1 at the origin to 0 at the extreme. The 0.71 level becomes the primary point of interest under the model.

The trader then defines the invalidation around 0.95 and identifies TP1 at 0 and TP2 at -0.21. The exact trade is not complete until the risk percentage is known. This ordering matters because it prevents the entry from being chosen first and the rest of the plan being reverse-engineered around it.

Direct entry versus refinement

The framework can distinguish a direct 0.71 entry from a refined entry using a liquidity sweep or additional confirmation. These are not automatically “better” or “worse.” They are different execution conditions. A direct entry can provide earlier participation but may face more unconfirmed risk. A refined entry can provide additional evidence but may miss the move if price does not return after the confirmation.

This is why a trading plan should record the exact entry type. If all trades are mixed together, the trader cannot later determine whether the direct model or refined model actually contributes to results. Data turns opinion into something testable.

Pre-trade checklist

  • Is H4/H1 context clearly defined?
  • Is price at a meaningful location rather than the middle of nowhere?
  • Is there a valid external or internal liquidity reference?
  • Has the required M15 structure confirmation closed?
  • Is the Fibonacci impulse objectively defined?
  • Is 0.71 aligned with the setup logic?
  • Is invalidation defined around 0.95?
  • Are TP1 0 and TP2 -0.21 logical for the current structure?
  • Is the predetermined risk percentage acceptable?
  • Would you take the same setup if the previous trade had been a winner?

Post-trade review

After the trade, record screenshots before and after execution. Note whether the setup followed the plan, whether entry was early, whether the structure definition was consistent, and whether the stop or target was changed. Do not judge the quality of the process solely by whether the trade won. A rule-following loss can be useful data. A rule-breaking winner can reinforce a dangerous habit.

The final rule is the simplest: no setup means no trade. A trader does not need to participate every day. If the required conditions are absent, waiting is the correct execution of the plan. Over time, the goal is not more trades; it is a cleaner sample of trades that actually belong to the model.

07 · ADVANCED CONCEPT

AMD — Accumulation, Manipulation & Distribution

AMD is best understood as a framework for studying how price can transition from balance into an expansion. The three words are useful because they describe different behavioural phases: accumulation represents a period in which price rotates or compresses and liquidity becomes increasingly visible; manipulation represents a move that raids or tests liquidity and may trap participants; distribution represents the subsequent delivery or expansion away from the balance area. The framework is powerful as a lens, but it should never be treated as a guarantee that every range must produce a sweep and reversal.

ACCUMULATION

Balance, compression, repeated tests, visible range boundaries and developing liquidity.

MANIPULATION

One-sided raid, failed breakout, liquidity sweep or displacement that changes the immediate structure.

DISTRIBUTION

Directional delivery away from the range toward the next meaningful objective or liquidity area.

1. Accumulation: where the market becomes obvious

Accumulation begins with balance. Price spends time moving back and forth rather than delivering continuously in one direction. The range may be tight or broad. What matters is that boundaries become identifiable. Repeated highs can create an obvious buy-side reference; repeated lows can create an obvious sell-side reference. Traders watching the chart can therefore begin to form expectations around the range.

At this stage, the correct behaviour is observation. Do not assume the upper boundary will be sold or the lower boundary will be bought. The market has not yet provided the information needed for that conclusion. Instead, mark the range, identify the most obvious external points, and watch how price interacts with them.

2. Manipulation: the liquidity event

The manipulation phase is where AMD becomes especially interesting. Price may push beyond a range boundary, triggering breakout orders and stop losses, then return inside the range. This can create the appearance that the breakout has failed. In other situations, price can sweep a previous high or low and then produce a strong displacement in the opposite direction. The event is meaningful because it tests whether the market can sustain acceptance beyond the level.

However, a sweep is not enough. If price trades above a high and closes above it with continued bullish delivery, calling the move “manipulation” after the fact would be hindsight. The trader needs a defined confirmation process. In the MA framework, that can mean waiting for a closed M15 structure shift and displacement after the liquidity event.

3. Distribution: delivery after confirmation

Distribution is the expansion phase. Once price leaves balance with enough evidence to support the thesis, it can travel toward another area of interest. This is where target selection matters. The trader should not simply assume that the move will continue indefinitely. Identify the next meaningful liquidity or structural objective and use the model's defined targets where applicable.

AMD across timeframes

AMD can exist on different scales. A large H4 accumulation can contain many M15 accumulation-manipulation sequences. This fractal behaviour is one reason timeframe context matters. A small M15 sweep can be a meaningful execution event while still occurring inside a larger H1 pullback. The trader should know which AMD cycle is being analyzed rather than mixing every range on the screen into one story.

AMD and the MA strategy

The MA model does not require AMD as a decorative label. AMD can help organize the sequence: identify the balance, observe the liquidity raid, wait for structure confirmation, identify the impulse, apply Fibonacci from 1 at origin to 0 at extreme, and evaluate the 0.71 point of interest. If the setup then reaches the defined invalidation around 0.95, the thesis is wrong and the trade ends. If price delivers, TP1 at 0 and TP2 at -0.21 provide predefined objectives.

Three AMD mistakes to eliminate

  • Forcing the pattern: not every range is accumulation and not every breakout is manipulation.
  • Entering on the sweep alone: the liquidity event needs the confirmation required by the strategy.
  • Using hindsight: the chart must be evaluated as it looked before the outcome was known.

The professional way to study AMD

Collect examples. Mark the range before the liquidity event. Record which side was swept. Record whether price returned inside or accepted beyond the boundary. Record whether displacement occurred. Record whether M15 structure shifted. Then record the distance to the Fibonacci 0.71 area, the stop location, TP1, TP2 and the eventual outcome. Separate clean examples from ambiguous ones instead of forcing every chart into the model.

AMD becomes valuable when it gives the trader a structured question: “Is price balancing, has it just tested liquidity, and has there been enough evidence of directional delivery to justify an execution model?” That question is far more useful than memorizing a picture. The goal of the course is to make you capable of explaining why a setup qualifies, why another does not, and exactly where your interpretation becomes invalid.

Important

AMD is a framework for interpreting price behaviour, not a certainty machine. A sweep can fail, a breakout can continue, and a distribution phase can reverse. Every setup still requires defined confirmation and risk.
